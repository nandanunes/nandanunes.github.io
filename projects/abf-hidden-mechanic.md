## Overview

Angry Birds Friends levels got bigger and more complex with time, with new blocks, mechanics, camera zoom and intricate puzzles. The Trigger’s design could no longer communicate its behavior and intent. I redesigned the Trigger pack (the Trigger, the Wires, the Landmine and the Sign) and rewrote how Level Designers use it.

## Outcome and impact

**For players:** the Trigger is now a pig-sized, colored, blinking object that stands out in a busy level, and the puzzles are clearer. Matching colors, and an optional Sign, show what it connects to and what to expect, so players can notice it before the first shot and plan around it. Off-screen results still stay a surprise.

**For Level Designers:** one set of rules replaces case-by-case choices, so levels are more consistent. The new colors, elements and size give designers more flexibility to work with Triggers.

**The decisions together:** color and an optional Sign tell players what a Trigger connects to, without spoiling the surprise. A pig-sized, blinking Trigger makes it part of the player’s plan. And rules of thumb, instead of a rigid rulebook, leave Level Designers room to experiment, as long as the intention and the result stay easy for the player to understand.

## The challenge

The starting problem was “objects on screen are too small for mobile”. But level complexity, camera zoom and player feedback also played a part, so I reframed it as “players have difficulty identifying level objects on mobile.” That changed the goal from making things bigger to making them readable.

For Triggers, this showed up in four ways:

- The control panel and light were small and hard to see on smaller screens.
- Nothing told the player what a Trigger would do, or what it was connected to.
- The Trigger was not part of the player’s decision making when solving the puzzle.
- Level Designers had no convention, so some Triggers had hints and some didn’t.

Levels that are easy to read can improve retention and replayability, and shared conventions keep levels consistent and make onboarding easier.

## Key decisions

### 1. Communicate the connection and the behavior, without spoiling the surprise.

Color helps link a Trigger to its counterpart, as Terraria’s wires and Super Mario’s switches show, but color alone was not enough in our levels. A visual sign next to the color helps the player connect both. Signs in Portal, Dead Cells and real life show that a small pictogram lets people predict what will happen, so the player can strategize better. The Sign is a last resort, so hints don’t spoil the surprise.

### 2. Make the Trigger part of the player’s plan.

I wrote six requirements for the Trigger:

- Stand out with a “poppable feel”, like Pigs.
- Get the player’s attention.
- Have a simple, recognizable shape.
- Offer a range of colors.
- Be the size of a medium pig.
- Show with a visual effect that it is active.

The playtest then settled three things:

- **Shape:** the most Mario-like one.
- **Colors:** red, yellow, blue and pink. Green was dropped so it would not be confused with the Pigs.
- **Blinking rate:** tuned so the Trigger gets noticed without being too crazy.

![Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit.](abf-hidden-mechanic-decision-2|16:9)

### 3. Give Level Designers rules of thumb, not a rigid rulebook.

The main rule is that a Trigger must clearly indicate what it activates, even if the outcome stays hidden. I left room to experiment, as long as the intention and the result are still easy for the player to understand.
