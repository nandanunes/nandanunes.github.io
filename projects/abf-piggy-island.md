## Overview

Angry Birds Friends is the closest title to the classic game, yet it didn’t feel like the Angry Birds world. Over time, many decisions that each made sense on their own added up to distance from the brand. I led a direction to bring the IP back to the center: backgrounds based on real Piggy Island locations, designed for readability, and a set of unique pig characters with guidelines for using them in levels.

## The challenge

Two problems pointed to the same root cause.

- **Accessibility.** Players complained that backgrounds were so bright and busy that the level layout blended in. Support complaints traced back to too many colors and low contrast, and some said pigs didn’t feel “poppable” because they didn’t stand out. Research also showed that players found the same background boring over time.
- **Identity.** The seasonal backgrounds weren’t tied to any real location, and spring and summer were nearly identical. Only one pig (the King) had a unique identity, despite the large cast in the Toons and comics.

Together they pointed to a layering and hierarchy problem across the whole level, not to a single asset. We also expected players to see the core game more often, so it had to be more appealing and less repetitive.

The evidence behind the direction:

- **Store reviews.** Backgrounds so bright that the course blended in, to the point that some players stopped playing.
- **Player support.** Repeated complaints about too many colors and low contrast, and pigs that did not stand out.
- **Research studies.** The same complaints, plus players calling the repeated background boring.

## Key decisions

### 1. Ground every choice in the lore, instead of inventing a new identity.

Earlier attempts to create an identity without brand guidance left visual variety but no coherence. I went back to the sources (the Toons, which defined the island’s map, plus the comics and official guides) and chose Piggy Island’s most iconic locations as the basis.

### 2. Make backgrounds readable first, and let variety come from biomes.

I reduced detail to focus on shape and clarity, so the background stops competing with the level. Variety now comes from different locations, not from recoloring the same scene by season. Constraining the environment direction also gave artists a clearer frame to work within.

### 3. Make pigs narrative anchors, with rules that keep them meaningful.

I introduced unique pigs from the lore, such as the Foreman, the Corporal and the Mechanic. They are bigger than minions and carry a distinct personality, but they don’t change how a level plays, so levels stay fair and readable. To keep them special, only one copy of a unique pig can appear per level.

For each pig I wrote guidelines that translate personality into level placement, so a level reads as if the pigs built it:

- **Foreman (the builder):** shown inside towers and structures, in scenes like a half-built fortress or a tea break among unfinished work. He can wear construction hats and hold tools, and he is no longer used as a plain minion.
- **Corporal (the commander):** placed near helmet pig troops, in scenes like an obstacle course where minions crash or a fortified command post. He can hold tools but not wear decoration.
- **Mechanic (the engineer):** shown repairing or operating machines and triggers, or helping the Foreman. Level Designers must always put a tool in his hand.

Older levels follow the new rules when they are next updated, so there was no need to rebuild everything at once.
